# Two models for getting to agreement

Read this when Step 1 (position versus interest) has left you with two parties who both want a deal and cannot find the shape of it.

## Schelling point

**What it is.** When two sides cannot coordinate by talking, they still converge, because some options are obvious in a way both can see the other seeing. Thomas Schelling's original case: two people told to meet in New York on a given day, with no way to communicate, disproportionately pick noon at Grand Central. Nothing makes that spot correct. It is simply the one each expects the other to expect.

**Where it shows up in a negotiation.**

- Round numbers. Prices settle on 100 rather than 97, splits on 50/50 rather than 53/47, not because those are fair but because they are obvious, and obvious is defensible to whoever each side has to report back to.
- Industry standard terms. Net 30, a 20% deposit, a three-month notice period. These carry no magic, but proposing one costs the other side nothing to accept, while proposing net 37 invites a whole conversation.
- Existing precedent. What you charged them last time. What they pay their other vendors.

**How to use it.** When a negotiation is stuck between two numbers, look for the focal point near the gap rather than splitting the difference. A split reads as arbitrary and invites another split. A focal point reads as a place to stop.

**How it goes wrong.** The obvious point is not the fair point. 50/50 between two parties who contributed very differently is a focal point pretending to be a principle. If the focal point is against you, name what makes this case different before it becomes the anchor, because once it is on the table it is very hard to move.

## Positive-sum games

**What it is.** A deal where both sides end up better off than they started, as opposed to dividing a fixed amount where every gain is someone's loss. Most business relationships are positive-sum and get negotiated as if they were not.

**How to find the positive-sum version.** Interests differ, and that is what makes trade possible. The pieces each side values differently are where the surplus is.

- **Time.** You need cash this quarter, they need the work done before their launch. Prepayment for priority scheduling costs you both less than it gives you both.
- **Risk.** They are unsure it will work. A smaller pilot at a lower rate costs you a discount and buys you a reference customer.
- **Scope.** They asked for everything because they did not know what they needed. Half the scope at 70% of the price can be better for both than the full thing at a grudging discount.
- **Visibility.** A case study, an intro, a logo on your site. Cheap for them, valuable to you, and it never comes up unless you ask.

**How to use it.** Before conceding on price, list what each side values differently. If you find nothing, the deal really is distributive and you are back to walkaways. Usually you find two or three things.

**How it goes wrong.** "Let's make the pie bigger" is also what someone says when they are avoiding a real conflict of interest. If the only thing in dispute is how much money moves from them to you, calling it positive-sum is a way of not saying no. Some deals are zero-sum and the honest move is to negotiate them as such.
