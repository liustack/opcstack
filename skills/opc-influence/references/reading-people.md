# Reading the other side, and what moves them

Three layers, used in order. Read them before Step 2 of `SKILL.md` (working out what they are guarding), and again before Step 4 (shaping the ask).

Everything here describes how a true case reaches someone. None of it is a licence to manufacture one. A move that only works when the underlying claim is invented is not a weaker version of persuasion, it is a different act, and it costs the relationship the moment it surfaces.

## Layer 1: read them

### Which level the pressure is at

Maslow's ordering, used as a diagnostic rather than a theory: a person worried about keeping their job does not hear an argument about growth or fulfilment. The pitch has to meet the level they are actually operating at.

- **Heard as**: a mismatch between your argument and their objection. You talk about upside, they keep returning to risk, headcount, or what their boss will say.
- **Do**: name the level out loud in your own notes, then re-pitch at it. Safety talks to safety.

### What they actually experience

The empathy pass: what do they see all day, who do they hear from, what gets them praised, what gets them blamed.

- **Heard as**: nothing. This is the one you have to run deliberately, because its absence sounds like a perfectly reasonable proposal that keeps not landing.
- **Do**: answer four questions before writing anything. What does their week look like. Who do they have to justify this to. What have they been burned by before. What would make this easy for them to say yes to.

### Why a good argument produces resistance

Cognitive dissonance: when your new fact collides with a belief they have acted on publicly, the collision is painful, and defending is cheaper than updating.

- **Heard as**: an argument that gets stronger the more evidence you add. Defensiveness that scales with how right you are.
- **Do**: give them a version where agreeing does not mean having been wrong. "The situation changed" costs them nothing. "You were mistaken for two years" costs them everything, and they will pay a lot to avoid it.

## Layer 2: earn the standing to be heard

### Skin in the game

Advice from someone who bears none of the downside is worth what it costs. If you carry risk alongside them, say what it is, concretely.

- **Do**: name your exposure. A guarantee, a milestone-linked fee, a rollback commitment. If you have no exposure to offer, notice that, because they have.

### Liking and authority

Both are real shortcuts and both are cheap to fake, which is why faking them is where reputations go. Genuine common ground and a track record you can produce on request are the versions that hold.

- **Do**: establish relevant credentials once, early, in a sentence, and let the work carry the rest. Repeating them reads as insecurity, and inflating them ends the relationship when checked.

### Handling the emotion before the problem

A person who is angry, cornered, or embarrassed is not evaluating your proposal.

- **Heard as**: short replies, sudden formality, cc'ing someone new.
- **Do**: address the state before the substance. One sentence acknowledging what happened, then a pause, then the ask. Skipping this makes every later paragraph unread.

## Layer 3: shape the ask

### Start from why

Open with what changes for them, not with what you are proposing or how it works. The order is why, then how, then what.

- **Do**: write the first line as the outcome they get. If it starts with your product, your rate, or your process, rewrite it.

### Reciprocity

Give something real first, and the door opens. This works because it is real, not because it is a technique.

- **Do**: send the useful thing before you need anything. A relevant intro, a fix you noticed, the answer to a question they have not asked yet.
- **Careful**: an obviously transactional gift creates debt rather than goodwill, and people resent debt.

### Commitment and consistency

People move in the direction of positions they have already taken, especially in public.

- **Do**: find the agreement that already exists and build from it. "You said reliability matters more this quarter" is a foundation. Manufacturing a small yes purely to leverage it later is manipulation and reads as such.

### Social proof and scarcity

Both only work when true, and both are the fastest way to lose a deal when not.

- **Do**: cite adoption you could produce on request, and deadlines that exist whether or not this conversation happens. The quarter closing is real. A deadline invented to hurry someone is a lie with a countdown on it.

### Growth mindset in feedback

Praising the approach invites more of it. Praising the person invites defensiveness the next time something goes wrong.

- **Do**: name what they did that worked, not what they are. This matters most with contractors and partners you want to keep.

### Story as the wrapper

Data makes people evaluate, a story makes them act. The structure that carries: a situation, something that changed, what it cost, what happens next.

- **Do**: put the why into a two-sentence story with a real before and after. If you cannot name the before, you do not have the case yet.

## Before you send

- Can you deliver what this ask commits you to?
- Would every claim survive them finding out how it was assembled?
- Does their yes survive you leaving the room, or does it need you standing there?
